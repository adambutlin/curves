Research note · October 2026

What drove the 2026 Treasury selloff?

A structural cross-asset decomposition of the US Treasury curve, from a Bayesian VAR identified with sign restrictions (Brandt et al., 2021), frozen at end-2025 and applied to 2026 without re-estimation.

Adam Butlin · Data to 5 October 2026 · Code and data

Between the closes of 30 December 2025 and 5 October 2026 the 10-year Treasury yield rose 117bp and the 2-year 139bp. At the long end, the rise was news that also moved equities, the dollar and euro-area rates, and US news was its largest source in 80% of admissible models (median 76bp of the 10-year's rise). The front end was different: about 61bp of the 2-year's rise was a repricing of the expected policy path that no cross-asset shock explains, and that repricing accounts for the whole of the curve's bear flattening. The decomposition explains most of each day's move but has no value for forecasting the next.

1. The 10-year: US news, plus a lift from risk sentiment

Yields fell into late February: by the 27 February low the 10-year was −17bp on the year, mostly on weaker US news. They then rose in three steps, in March, July and, most sharply, September (+54bp). In the representative model (the median-target model of Fry and Pagan, the single admissible model closest to the median impulse responses), US news accounts for 96bp of the 117bp rise, improving global risk sentiment for 21bp, and euro-area news for almost nothing (−1bp). A positive risk-sentiment contribution means risk appetite improved: the safe-haven demand for Treasuries unwound and yields rose. Together the five shocks leave essentially none of the 10-year's rise unexplained.

Across all admissible models the 5–95% range of the US contribution is 31–119bp, with a median of 76bp. Its sign is certain; its size is not.

2. Across the curve: the same news at the long end, a policy repricing at the front

At 5, 10 and 30 years the cross-asset shocks account for nearly all of the rise. At 2 years they account for only about half: 61bp of the 139bp (44–80bp across admissible models) is news that moved the 2-year without the equity, currency and euro-area signature of any identified shock. The NY Fed's term-structure model (Adrian, Crump and Moench) places that part in expected short rates rather than in the term premium: 66bp of the 2-year's 99bp rise in expected rates is unspanned, against −10bp of its 36bp rise in the term premium. It is a repricing of the expected path of the federal funds rate, the kind of news carried by data releases and Fed communication that markets price mainly at the front end.

Every identified shock steepens the curve when it raises yields: its 10-year loading exceeds its 2-year loading in at least 83% of admissible models for the US and global shocks. The cross-asset news therefore steepened 2s10s, by 41bp (31–50), and the 22bp bear flattening came entirely from the unspanned front-end repricing (−63bp; −72 to −53). At the long end, 10s30s flattened by 32bp, partly because US news moves 5- to 10-year yields most (−12bp) and partly through unspanned news (−19bp).

3. When it happened: three up-legs, each led by US news

The selloff was not one move. At the 10-year it came in March (+33bp; US news the largest origin in 87% of admissible models), July (+31bp; 65%) and September (+54bp; 86%). The dominant driver did not change: US news led every leg. In the quiet months the small moves are attributed mainly to risk sentiment in the representative model, but there the identification cannot tell global from euro-area news. At the 2-year the unspanned repricing came in the same bursts, adding +19bp in March, +13bp in June and +21bp in September.

4. How certain is the attribution?

Sign restrictions identify a set of models, not one: 1,000 rotations of the reduced form satisfy the restrictions and each is an equally admissible account of the same data. Figure 4 sorts the results by how much they depend on which rotation is right.

  • Identification-free. How much of a move is spanned by the cross-asset shocks does not depend on the restrictions at all. The 10-year's unspanned part is −2bp (−25 to 25), the 2-year's 61bp (44–80).
  • Robust in sign. US news raised yields at every maturity in every admissible model. It is the largest of the three origins in 80% of models at the 10-year and 88% at the 2-year, and accounts for more than half of the 10-year's rise in 72%.
  • Weakly identified. The remainder is split between global risk sentiment (median 24bp, −2 to 74) and euro-area news (median 11bp, −1 to 47) in proportions the data do not pin down. The representative model's zero for euro-area news lies at the bottom of the set, so "no euro-area contribution" is not a robust finding.
  • Not pinned down. US macro news exceeds US monetary news in 53% of models. The two shocks differ only in the sign of the US equity response, and the data cannot tell whether the US news of 2026 was mainly about growth or about policy.

5. Does knowing the cause predict what happens next?

No. The loadings frozen at end-2025 explain 59%–92% of the daily variance of 2026 yield changes, as much as in 2007–2025, so the structure was stable through the selloff. But in real-time forecasts over 2012–2025, with the VAR, the identification and the loadings re-estimated each year on past data only, adding the origin of today's news to the day's own move and the state of the curve never improves forecasts of the change over the next 1, 5 or 20 days (Clark–West p ≥ 0.11 at every maturity and horizon), and no specification beats a no-change forecast. 2026 gives the same answer.

One apparent exception is an artefact. Measured from the same close, today's news "predicts" tomorrow's 10-year H.15 change (out-of-sample R² +1.3%, p < 0.001). H.15 yields are recorded earlier in the New York afternoon than the market prices the model uses, so tomorrow's H.15 change contains the end of today's news. The effect disappears when the forecast starts at the next close, and it is not tradeable.

The decomposition is an attribution and state-classification tool, not a forecasting model. It says what kind of news moved yields, robustly at the level of origin, and nothing about where yields go next.

Method

Model. Brandt et al.'s (2021) daily Bayesian VAR in five variables, recorded at or near the New York close: the 10-year euro-area OIS rate (EONIA, then €STR), the Euro Stoxx 50 future, the S&P 500, the euro-dollar exchange rate and the euro-area minus US 10-year spread. Estimated on 2007–2025 (4,673 days) with four lags and a Minnesota prior (overall tightness 0.2):

Yt = c + A1Yt−1 + … + A4Yt−4 + ut,    ut = Bεt,    E[εtεt′] = I,

where Yt holds the five daily changes, ut the reduced-form innovations with covariance Σ, and εt the five structural shocks. The impact matrix B = chol(Σ)Q is identified only up to the orthogonal rotation Q.

Identification. Sign restrictions on impact, exactly as in Brandt et al.'s Table 1. Rotations are drawn uniformly (Arias, Rubio-Ramírez and Waggoner, 2018) jointly with the reduced-form posterior until 1,000 satisfy the restrictions (2.5% of candidates). The spread restriction identifies the country of origin: a domestic shock moves the domestic long rate by more than the foreign one.

· = unrestricted. US monetary and US macro news both raise US yields and the dollar; they differ only in the sign of the S&P 500 response, which is why the split between them is fragile. A positive global-risk shock is a flight to safety, so a negative one (improving sentiment) raises Treasury yields.

The Treasury curve. The model contains one Treasury yield. The 2-, 5-, 10- and 30-year H.15 yields are attached by projecting each day's change on the five shocks and two lags, model by model, on 2007–2025 data:

Δyτ,t = cτ + Σk=15 Σs=02 θτ,k,s εk,t−s + eτ,t,

where Δyτ,t is the daily change in the τ-year yield. The lags credit the catch-up of H.15 yields, which are recorded before the New York close, to the shock that caused it. The contribution of shock k over a window W is Σt∈W Σs θτ,k,sεk,t−s; the rest of the window's change is "unspanned": news the five cross-asset shocks do not represent. Because the span of the shocks does not depend on the rotation, neither does the unspanned part.

Discipline. The model and the curve loadings were frozen at end-2025, hashed, and committed before any 2026 maturity data were read; the design was pre-registered. Nothing is re-estimated on 2026 data. On 2007–2025 the model reproduces Brandt et al.'s spillovers closely (US shocks explain 35% of the daily variance of the euro-area rate, against their 40%), and its median-target model attributes 15 of 18 of their dated events to the expected shock, although the average admissible model manages only 50%. In the full sample, US shocks account for 51% of the daily variance of the 10-year Treasury yield, global risk for 34% and euro-area shocks for 14%.

Limitations

  • Set identification. The 5–95% range of the US contribution to the 10-year spans 88bp. Robust statements concern the origin of the news and the unspanned front end; the macro/monetary split and the global/euro-area split are not robust.
  • The representative model is not central on every dimension. The median-target model used in Figures 1–3 sits near the upper end of the US-news range and at the bottom of the euro-area range; the medians across models are reported beside it throughout.
  • Timing. H.15 yields are recorded before the New York close; two lags in the projection handle this for attribution. LSEG benchmark yields at every maturity would remove the issue.
  • Unspanned is not noise. It is news the five cross-asset shocks do not represent. Reading the front-end part as a policy-path repricing relies on the NY Fed's term-structure model, itself an estimate.
  • Expectations versus term premium. Loadings estimated on 2007–2025, a sample in which the policy rate sat at its lower bound almost half the time, map cross-asset news mainly into the term premium; in 2026 the same news moved expected rates instead (the NY Fed's model puts 79% of the 10-year's rise in expected rates). A lower bound mutes the response of expected rates to news (Swanson and Williams, 2014), so any fixed mapping from this decomposition into expectations and premia is regime-dependent.
  • Data. The five model variables are LSEG data recorded at the New York close and cannot be redistributed. The figures and this page are rebuilt from derived tables that are published with the code.
